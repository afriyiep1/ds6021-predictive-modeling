6.1 Reserve data for evaluation

The aim of predictive modeling is performance on new observations. The best conditional-mean target under squared loss is f(x)=E[Y∣X=x]f(x)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]f(x)=E[Y∣X=x], but we learn an approximation from finite data.

Use training data to estimate parameters, validation data or cross-validation to make modeling choices, and an untouched test set for final assessment. Once test results influence a choice, that set has participated in selection. Calling it a test set does not undo that use.

In the class notebook, R&D spending (RD) is the only predictor of profit. A 70/30 split with seed 6021 leaves 35 training rows and 15 test rows. Small held-out samples can give noisy comparisons, so avoid presenting one split as a universal model ranking.

Preprocessing must also respect the split. Learn scaling, imputation, and transformations with fitted parameters from training data only, including within each cross-validation fold.

Match the split to the future task

A random split can be reasonable for exchangeable rows. If the goal is future prediction, training on later observations while testing on earlier ones can give an unrealistic assessment. If several rows belong to one store, patient, or location, a group split may be needed to evaluate performance on new units.

The correct split follows the intended deployment question. “New hour at a known location” and “new location never seen in training” are different tasks, even when they use the same weather table.

Original class setup: RD is the only predictor; test_size=0.30 and random_state=6021. This leaves 35 training and 15 test observations.

6.2 Let a polynomial bend

Polynomial regression adds powers of the predictor:

y^=b0+b1x+b2x2+⋯+bdxd.\widehat y=b_0+b_1x+b_2x^2+\cdots+b_dx^d.y​=b0​+b1​x+b2​x2+⋯+bd​xd.

It is nonlinear as a curve in xxx, but linear in its coefficients. Ordinary least squares can fit the expanded design matrix. Larger degree offers more flexibility and can create unstable behavior, especially near boundaries and beyond the observed range.

For a quadratic, the local slope is b1+2b2xb_1+2b_2xb1​+2b2​x. The exact change from xxx to x+1x+1x+1 is instead b1+b2(2x+1)b_1+b_2(2x+1)b1​+b2​(2x+1). A coefficient on xxx alone is therefore not a constant one-unit effect when powers of xxx are also present.

Nested polynomial models cannot increase minimum training SSE when fitted by unconstrained OLS. Their test error can increase. The notebook compares a line with degree-3 and degree-8 polynomials using the same training rows.

Linear in coefficients does not mean straight in the input

The degree-ddd design has columns 1,x,x2,…,xd1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^d1,x,x2,…,xd. Once those columns are constructed, fitting is the same coefficient-estimation problem used earlier. The curvature comes from the chosen basis.

Centering and scaling can help numerical stability because high powers of large raw values can have extreme magnitudes. Fit that scaling on the training set and reuse it for test points. Refitting a scaler separately on test data changes the meaning of the inputs supplied to the fitted model.

6.3 Use a spline basis

A spline joins polynomial pieces at knots. It permits local changes in shape while enforcing smoothness at joins. A simple linear-spline hinge is

hc(x)=(x−c)+=max⁡(x−c,0).h_c(x)=(x-c)_+=\max(x-c,0).hc​(x)=(x−c)+​=max(x−c,0).

A model containing xxx and such hinges can change slope at knot locations while remaining continuous. Cubic splines use cubic pieces with continuity of the function and its first two derivatives at interior knots. A natural cubic spline adds boundary conditions that produce linear tails.

More generally, a basis expansion has form

f^(x)=b0+∑j=1qbjBj(x).\widehat f(x)=b_0+\sum_{j=1}^q b_jB_j(x).f​(x)=b0​+j=1∑q​bj​Bj​(x).

The class code uses a B-spline basis with degree=3, n_knots=8, and default constant extrapolation. It is not a natural cubic spline with linear tails. Knot count and polynomial degree are different controls, not interchangeable positions on a universal complexity scale.

Local flexibility and extrapolation

Polynomial powers affect the fitted curve globally. Spline bases can express more local adjustments around knots. This does not make a spline automatically superior: too much flexibility can still fit noise, and boundary behavior remains a modeling choice.

In the class pipeline, constant extrapolation means the basis is held at its boundary value outside the training range. That produces different behavior from a natural cubic spline's linear tails. Inspect the library settings and the plotted domain before describing what a model predicts beyond observed R&D spending.

The original spline uses degree=3, n_knots=8, and the default constant extrapolation. Polynomial degree and spline knot count do not define one universal complexity scale.

6.4 Evaluate predictions on the test set

Evaluate all candidates on the same held-out outcomes. Root mean squared error uses response units:

RMSE⁡=1ntest∑i∈test(yi−y^i)2.\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1{n_{\mathrm{test}}}\sum_{i\in\mathrm{test}}(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}.RMSE=ntest​1​i∈test∑​(yi​−y​i​)2​.

Test R2R^2R2 compares these squared errors with deviations from the test-response mean. It can be negative. An observed-versus-predicted plot adds information about systematic overprediction, underprediction, and unusually large errors that one score cannot show.

Cross-validation

Split the training pool into KKK folds. Fit on all but one fold, predict that held-out fold, and repeat. Pooling all out-of-fold squared errors gives

CV MSE⁡=1n∑k=1K∑i∈Ik(yi−f^(−k)(xi))2.\operatorname{CV\ MSE}=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{i\in I_k}\left(y_i-\widehat f^{(-k)}(x_i)\right)^2.CV MSE=n1​k=1∑K​i∈Ik​∑​(yi​−f​(−k)(xi​))2.

An unweighted mean of fold MSEs is equivalent only when fold sizes are equal. Fit preprocessing inside each fold. After choosing a model, refit it on the training pool and evaluate once on the untouched test data. The displayed original notebook uses one split; these cells do not run cross-validation.

Read RMSE and test R² together

On the same test observations,

Rtest2=1−∑i(yi−y^i)2∑i(yi−y‾test)2.R^2_{\mathrm{test}}=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\overline y_{\mathrm{test}})^2}.Rtest2​=1−∑i​(yi​−y​test​)2∑i​(yi​−y​i​)2​.

RMSE measures the typical scale of squared-error discrepancies in profit units. Test R2R^2R2 expresses error relative to the variation of that particular test set. A negative value means the numerator exceeds that test-mean benchmark; it does not mean correlation is negative.

The test mean is used to define this score, not as a deployable prediction learned from training. A separate practical baseline can predict the training mean for every test case. Reporting that baseline makes the predictive comparison easier to understand.

The displayed notebook evaluates one train/test split; it imports cross-validation utilities but does not perform cross-validation in these cells. Selecting models using these test results would make this a selection set.

6.5 Separate bias, variance, and prediction uncertainty

What varies across training samples?

At a fixed input x0x_0x0​, imagine repeatedly drawing training datasets DDD and fitting f^D\widehat f_Df​D​. Bias compares the average fitted value with the true conditional mean; variance describes the spread of fitted values:

Bias⁡(x0)=ED[f^D(x0)]−f(x0),\operatorname{Bias}(x_0)=\mathbb E_D[\widehat f_D(x_0)]-f(x_0),Bias(x0​)=ED​[f​D​(x0​)]−f(x0​), Var⁡D[f^D(x0)]=ED[(f^D(x0)−EDf^D(x0))2].\operatorname{Var}_D[\widehat f_D(x_0)]=\mathbb E_D\left[(\widehat f_D(x_0)-\mathbb E_D\widehat f_D(x_0))^2\right].VarD​[f​D​(x0​)]=ED​[(f​D​(x0​)−ED​f​D​(x0​))2].

For an independent new response with zero-mean noise at x0x_0x0​,

ED,Y0[(Y0−f^D(x0))2∣x0]=Bias⁡(x0)2+Var⁡D[f^D(x0)]+Var⁡(ε0∣x0).\mathbb E_{D,Y_0}\left[(Y_0-\widehat f_D(x_0))^2\mid x_0\right]=\operatorname{Bias}(x_0)^2+\operatorname{Var}_D[\widehat f_D(x_0)]+\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_0\mid x_0).ED,Y0​​[(Y0​−f​D​(x0​))2∣x0​]=Bias(x0​)2+VarD​[f​D​(x0​)]+Var(ε0​∣x0​).

To see the decomposition, add and subtract f(x0)f(x_0)f(x0​) and EDf^D(x0)\mathbb E_D\widehat f_D(x_0)ED​f​D​(x0​) inside the error. Expand the square. The centered estimator and zero-mean independent noise make the cross terms vanish. The remaining terms are squared bias, estimator variance, and irreducible noise.

Flexible models often reduce approximation bias while becoming more sensitive to training samples. This is a tendency, not a guarantee of a particular ordering for every dataset or fitting method. We do not know the true fff in the startups example, so its true squared bias is not directly observable.

Mean uncertainty versus individual uncertainty

A confidence interval for f(x0)f(x_0)f(x0​) concerns the mean response. A prediction interval for a new Y0Y_0Y0​ also includes outcome noise and is generally wider. Bootstrap refits can illustrate estimator variability; adding resampled residuals introduces an additional model for individual noise. The class cell at RD = 150,000 uses this illustrative approach, whose assumptions and limitations matter.

Why more data can help one interval more than another

Imagine knowing the conditional mean almost perfectly at a chosen R&D level. A confidence interval for the mean could become very narrow. A prediction interval for a new firm's profit would still need to account for firm-to-firm variation around that mean.

The irreducible-noise term is conditional on the chosen information. Adding a useful predictor can explain some previously unmodeled variation, changing the conditional distribution. “Irreducible” therefore refers to uncertainty given the predictor set and problem definition, not a claim that no future information could ever help.

Bootstrap variability measures sensitivity to resampled training data. It cannot reveal true bias without additional information about the unknown response function. Label empirical bootstrap spread as an estimate of variability, not as an observed bias–variance decomposition.

These are illustrative percentile bootstrap intervals. Adding sampled training residuals assumes a common error distribution and can underestimate individual uncertainty, especially for flexible fits. They are not guaranteed 95% coverage, and the true bias² is unknown here.

rows = []

for name, model in models.items():


    y_hat = model.predict(X_new).item()


    residuals = y_train_array - model.predict(X_train).ravel()
    residuals = residuals - residuals.mean()

    mean_predictions = []
    individual_predictions = []

    for b in range(B):


        idx = rng.choice(
            len(X_train), size=len(X_train), replace=True
        )

        boot_model = clone(model)
        boot_model.fit(X_train.iloc[idx], y_train_array[idx])

        boot_prediction = boot_model.predict(X_new).item()


        mean_predictions.append(boot_prediction)


        random_error = rng.choice(residuals)
        individual_predictions.append(boot_prediction + random_error)

    ci_lower, ci_upper = np.percentile(
        mean_predictions, [2.5, 97.5]
    )

    pi_lower, pi_upper = np.percentile(
        individual_predictions, [2.5, 97.5]
    )

    rows.append({
        "Model": name,
        "Predicted Profit": y_hat,
        "95% CI Lower": ci_lower,
        "95% CI Upper": ci_upper,

    })

prediction_results = pd.DataFrame(rows)

6.6 Connect flexibility to regularization

Regularization adds a penalty to the training objective. With the intercept unpenalized, ridge and lasso use

Ridge:min⁡b0,b{1n∑i(yi−b0−xiTb)2+λ∑jbj2},Lasso:min⁡b0,b{1n∑i(yi−b0−xiTb)2+λ∑j∣bj∣}.\begin{aligned}\text{Ridge:}\quad&\min_{b_0,\mathbf b}\left\{\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-b_0-\mathbf x_i^\mathsf T\mathbf b)^2+\lambda\sum_j b_j^2\right\},\\\text{Lasso:}\quad&\min_{b_0,\mathbf b}\left\{\frac1n\sum_i(y_i-b_0-\mathbf x_i^\mathsf T\mathbf b)^2+\lambda\sum_j|b_j|\right\}.\end{aligned}Ridge:Lasso:​b0​,bmin​{n1​i∑​(yi​−b0​−xiT​b)2+λj∑​bj2​},b0​,bmin​{n1​i∑​(yi​−b0​−xiT​b)2+λj∑​∣bj​∣}.​

Ridge shrinks coefficients; lasso can set some exactly to zero. Predictor scales affect penalties, so standardization is typically part of the training pipeline. The tuning parameter λ\lambdaλ controls the tradeoff and should be selected using validation or cross-validation, not a repeatedly consulted final test set.

A smoothing spline controls curvature directly:

min⁡f{∑i(yi−f(xi))2+λ∫[f′′(t)]2 dt}.\min_f\left\{\sum_i(y_i-f(x_i))^2+\lambda\int[f''(t)]^2\,dt\right\}.fmin​{i∑​(yi​−f(xi​))2+λ∫[f′′(t)]2dt}.

A larger penalty discourages rapid bending. This illustrates a broader lesson: flexibility can be controlled through the basis, the number of parameters, or a penalty on the fitted function.

A complete modeling workflow

Define the prediction target and available inputs. Reserve evaluation data. Fit preprocessing and candidate models within training folds. Compare predictive error and diagnostics. Choose an appropriate complexity, refit, and assess on untouched data. Communicate the uncertainty, domain limitations, and meaning of the predictions alongside the score.

Penalization changes what “best fit” means

With centered data and a ridge objective using MSE plus λ∥b∥2\lambda\|\mathbf b\|^2λ∥b∥2, the slope coefficients satisfy

b^ridge=(XTX+nλI)−1XTy.\widehat{\mathbf b}_{\mathrm{ridge}}=(X^\mathsf TX+n\lambda I)^{-1}X^\mathsf T\mathbf y.bridge​=(XTX+nλI)−1XTy.

The factor nnn follows from using mean rather than summed squared error. Other conventions absorb it into the penalty parameter, so numerical values of λ\lambdaλ are not automatically comparable across implementations.

For positive λ\lambdaλ, ridge stabilizes directions that are weakly identified by the data, trading some bias for reduced sensitivity. Lasso's absolute-value penalty has a corner at zero, which helps explain why exact zeros can appear. Choose penalty strength within training-based validation.

Book connection: ISL develops resampling, shrinkage, and nonlinear bases in separate chapters. ESL unifies them through assessment of prediction error. Use that organization to distinguish fitting a flexible curve from demonstrating that it is useful.

                           PolynomialFeatures(8, include_bias=False),
                           StandardScaler(), LinearRegression()),
    "Spline": make_pipeline(SplineTransformer(n_knots=8, degree=3,
                                             include_bias=False),
                           StandardScaler(), LinearRegression())
}
# The first scaler helps numerical stability before polynomial expansion.
# The second standardizes the expanded features; it is not essential for OLS.
rows = []
grid = pd.DataFrame({"RD": np.linspace(X_train["RD"].min(), X_train["RD"].max(), 200)})
for name, model in models.items():
    model.fit(X_train, y_train)
    y_hat = model.predict(X_test)
    rows.append({"Model": name,
                 "Test RMSE": np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_test, y_hat)),
                 "Test R²": r2_score(y_test, y_hat)})
    fig = go.Figure()
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=X_train["RD"], y=y_train,
                             mode="markers", name="Train"))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=X_test["RD"], y=y_test,
                             mode="markers", name="Test"))
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=grid["RD"], y=model.predict(grid),
                             mode="lines", name=name))
    fig.update_layout(title=name, xaxis_title="RD", yaxis_title="Profit")
    fig.show()
    fig = px.scatter(x=y_test, y=y_hat,
                     labels={"x": "Observed profit", "y": "Predicted profit"},
                     title=f"{name}: test observations")
    limits = [min(y_test.min(), y_hat.min()), max(y_test.max(), y_hat.max())]
    fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=limits, y=limits, mode="lines",
                             name="Perfect prediction", line={"dash": "dash"}))
    fig.show()

results = pd.DataFrame(rows)
print(results.round(2))

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Does a degree-8 polynomial necessarily predict better than a degree-3 polynomial?

Reveal explanation

No. With the same training data and unpenalized least squares, the larger polynomial family cannot have a worse optimal training SSE (apart from numerical issues). Test error can increase because the fitted curve is sensitive to sample-specific variation. A spline is a different model family, not automatically the next step on a single complexity scale.

Optional practice questions

Want more practice? Try these questions after reading the chapter. They are for self-study; work through your reasoning before revealing an answer.

Reveal 5 practice questions

  1. Diagnose a model comparison

    A degree-8 polynomial has lower training RMSE than a line, but higher test RMSE. What does this suggest? Why would one 15-observation test set be insufficient to declare the line universally superior?

    Reveal suggested answer

    The flexible model may be fitting training-specific noise and generalizing less well on these held-out observations. A small test sample gives a noisy comparison, and the result depends on the sampled data and training procedure. Use validation or cross-validation within the training pool for selection, while keeping a final test set separate.

  2. Separate bias from variability

    At a fixed RD value, bootstrap predictions from a model are tightly clustered. What have you learned, and what would you still need to claim that the model has low true bias? Can true bias² be read directly from the startups data?

    Reveal suggested answer

    The cluster suggests low estimated resampling variability at that input under the bootstrap assumptions. Low true bias requires the average prediction across training samples to be close to the unknown conditional mean f(x). The startups observations do not reveal f(x), so true bias² is not directly observable there. A bootstrap bias estimate relative to a fitted statistic is not a direct measurement of prediction bias relative to f(x).

  3. Choose the right interval

    At RD = 150,000, distinguish a confidence interval for mean profit from a prediction interval for one new startup's profit. Why can the latter remain wide even with a very stable fitted mean?

    Reveal suggested answer

    The confidence interval concerns the conditional mean profit at that RD value. The prediction interval concerns a new individual outcome and includes both uncertainty in the fitted mean and individual outcome noise. Even a well-estimated mean cannot eliminate genuine variation among startups. Coverage still depends on the interval procedure and its assumptions.

  4. Keep validation honest

    A student scales the entire dataset, chooses polynomial degree using test RMSE, and then reports that same test RMSE as a final assessment. Identify two problems and outline a better workflow.

    Reveal suggested answer

    Scaling on the full data lets held-out observations influence preprocessing. Selecting degree using the test results makes that set part of model selection. Reserve the final test set first; fit scalers and candidate models within training-only validation folds; choose the degree; refit the selected pipeline on the training pool; then evaluate once on the final test set. The final test set should represent the intended prediction setting.

  5. Prepare for regularization

    A ridge penalty is increased while the intercept is unpenalized. Explain the intended bias–variance tradeoff. Why should predictor scaling and selection of the penalty strength be part of the training workflow?

    Reveal suggested answer

    A stronger penalty discourages large coefficients and typically reduces sensitivity to training-sample fluctuations at the cost of more shrinkage bias. It may improve test performance despite a worse training fit, but there is no guarantee for a particular dataset. Predictor units affect coefficient magnitudes and therefore the penalty; learn scaling within training folds and choose penalty strength by validation or cross-validation.
