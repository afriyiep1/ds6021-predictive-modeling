Hierarchical Clustering: Building Groups from the Bottom Up

K-means asks us to pick K first. Hierarchical clustering lets the data build a tree of nested groups, then lets us decide where to cut it.

← Interactive weather dashboard  ·  Open companion Python notebook ↗  ·  Print / Save as PDF

1. Why hierarchical clustering?

Imagine clustering weather hours by cloud cover, humidity, and precipitation. In K-means we choose K, initialize K centroids, and update memberships. But what if we do not know whether the right answer is two weather regimes or six? Rather than start by fixing K, hierarchical clustering builds a sequence of merges.

Agglomerative: each observation starts alone; at each step merge the two closest groups. Divisive: start with all observations together and split. We focus on agglomerative clustering, because it is widely used and easy to visualize.

Intuition: If 48 observations start alone, there are initially 48 groups. After one merge there are 47, after two there are 46, and after 47 merges there is one group. The whole history is a dendrogram.

The distance between A and B is 1, as is the distance between C and D. So the first two merges are A–B and C–D, in either order. Now the question becomes: how far apart are the two groups {A,B} and {C,D}?

Euclidean distance: d(x,y) = √[Σj=1p(xj−yj)²]

A pairwise distance measures separation between two observations. A linkage rule determines the separation between two clusters.

3. Linkage rules: Which groups should merge?

RuleCluster-to-cluster distanceIntuition
SingleSmallest cross-cluster pairwise distanceOne close bridge connects the groups; can chain
CompleteLargest cross-cluster pairwise distanceEven the farthest pair should not be too far apart
AverageMean of all cross-cluster pairwise distancesCompromise between extremes
WardMerge with the smallest increase in within-cluster SSEConnects to the K-means objective

For A,B versus C,D the four cross-group Euclidean distances are √18, 5, √13, √18. Thus:

Single = √13 ≈ 3.606  ·  Complete = 5  ·  Average ≈ 4.275

Both the data and the distance metric stayed the same. Only the linkage definition changed, and so the final dendrogram height changes.

Ward's method: for two clusters A,B of sizes nA, nB and means x̄A, x̄B,

ΔSSE(A,B) = [nAnB/(nA+nB)] ‖x̄A−x̄B‖².

Ward greedily chooses the merge with smallest ΔSSE. It resembles K-means in optimizing within-group compactness, but it never reassigns points after a merge. In SciPy, Ward dendrogram height is √(2 × ΔSSE), not ΔSSE itself.

4. How do we read a dendrogram?

Each leaf represents one observation. Branches show which groups join. The height of a merge indicates the linkage distance (or Ward height) at which it occurred. The left-to-right leaf order does not measure actual Euclidean separation; branches may be rotated without changing the clustering.

Interactive exercise: Open the Hierarchical tab and choose 24 hours. Click Next merge three times. Observe the groups disappear and the corresponding dendrogram branches highlight. Then click Complete tree, choose K = 2, 3, 4, and compare.

A horizontal cut through the tree produces clusters. Important: changing K changes the cut, not the original tree. The nesting is fixed by the chosen distance and linkage.

Look for large gaps between adjacent merger heights as one possible cut, but do not treat that as a proof of an optimal K.

5. How do we choose K?

The dendrogram is a visual diagnostic. Silhouette analysis adds a quantitative measure previously introduced for K-means. For an observation i, let a(i) be the average distance to points in its own group and b(i) the smallest average distance to points in another group:

s(i) = [b(i) − a(i)] / max{a(i), b(i)}.

Scores approach 1 for points well separated from other groups, 0 for boundary points, and become negative when points are on average closer to a different group. A singleton's silhouette is defined as 0 by convention.

Calculate mean silhouette for several cuts (K = 2,…,10), and inspect both the average-vs-K curve and the per-cluster silhouette profiles. A high average can conceal a small poorly separated group. Silhouette does not necessarily capture stability or domain usefulness.

6. Distance and scale: apply this to weather

The weather dashboard uses hourly model estimates for Charlottesville. In 2D, cloud cover and relative humidity are the distance features; in 3D, precipitation is added. Observations are sampled from the same weather series used in K-means, KNN, and multinomial logit tabs.

Raw Euclidean: large numerical scales can dominate. Z-score Euclidean: each numeric variable is centered and divided by its population standard deviation. These are modeling choices; standardization is often useful but is not mathematically mandatory.

Gower dissimilarity: compare mixed types by averaging normalized numeric differences and categorical mismatches:

dGower(i,j) = Σvwvδijv / Σvwv.

For numeric predictors, δ is the absolute difference divided by that variable's range. For nominal variables, δ is 0 on a match and 1 on a mismatch. Missing-value support requires excluding unavailable comparisons from the denominator; this dashboard uses complete cases only.

The dashboard adds a four-category daypart (night, morning, afternoon, evening) solely to demonstrate how categorical features can enter a distance matrix. The plotted 2D axes cannot display daypart, but it still contributes to the clustering.

Ward's SSE interpretation requires Euclidean feature geometry. Do not feed arbitrary Gower dissimilarities into Ward. Use single, complete, or average linkage for Gower.

7. How does hierarchical clustering compare to K-means?

QuestionK-meansHierarchical clustering
When do we choose K?Before fittingOften after building the tree
What changes during fitting?Centroids and memberships can update repeatedlyTwo clusters merge irreversibly at each step
Optimization connection?Minimizes within-cluster SSE locallyWard greedily minimizes SSE increase per merge
What output helps interpretation?Centroids and cluster profilesDendrogram and merge heights
New observation?Assign nearest fitted centroidRequires an extra assignment rule or refitting

Both approaches are unsupervised: meaningful-sounding labels such as “rainy hour” are interpretations, not ground truth used to train the clusters.
