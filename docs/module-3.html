3.1 See the relationship before fitting

Regression describes a quantitative response using predictors. Write

Y=f(X)+ε,E[ε∣X]=0.Y=f(X)+\varepsilon,\qquad \mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0.Y=f(X)+ε,E[ε∣X]=0.

Then f(x)=E[Y∣X=x]f(x)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]f(x)=E[Y∣X=x] is the conditional mean. A simple linear model approximates it by β0+β1x\beta_0+\beta_1xβ0​+β1​x. Population coefficients are unknown; fitted coefficients b0,b1b_0,b_1b0​,b1​ are computed from a sample.

In the planets dataset, distance is the predictor and revolution period is the response. Plot first: a line is a proposed approximation, and curvature suggests its form may be inadequate. The data include Pluto as a historical row, giving nine listed bodies.

The fitted rule is y^=b0+b1x\widehat y=b_0+b_1xy​=b0​+b1​x. The slope is the change in fitted response per unit increase in the predictor. The intercept is the fitted response at zero, which need not have a meaningful physical interpretation outside the relevant range.

A regression statement is conditional

The expression E[Y∣X=x]=β0+β1x\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]=\beta_0+\beta_1xE[Y∣X=x]=β0​+β1​x describes an average among comparable cases at a given predictor value. It does not say all observations lie on the line. The error term represents departures from that mean, including unmeasured influences and variation.

In the planets example, strong curvature is especially instructive: a precisely computed least-squares line can still be an inadequate approximation. Separate a statement about the numerical fit from one about the physical relationship.

3.2 Define loss and fit the OLS line

A residual is observed minus fitted:

ei=yi−y^i=yi−b0−b1xi.e_i=y_i-\widehat y_i=y_i-b_0-b_1x_i.ei​=yi​−y​i​=yi​−b0​−b1​xi​.

A positive residual means underprediction. Ordinary least squares minimizes squared errors. Dividing by nnn gives the same minimizer:

SSE⁡=∑i=1n(yi−b0−b1xi)2,J=SSE⁡n.\operatorname{SSE}=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2,\qquad J=\frac{\operatorname{SSE}}n.SSE=i=1∑n​(yi​−b0​−b1​xi​)2,J=nSSE​.

Squaring prevents cancellation, gives larger errors more influence, and produces a differentiable objective. It is a choice of loss, not the only possibility.

Derive the coefficients

Set the partial derivatives equal to zero:

∂SSE⁡∂b0=−2∑i(yi−b0−b1xi)=0,\frac{\partial\operatorname{SSE}}{\partial b_0}=-2\sum_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0,∂b0​∂SSE​=−2i∑​(yi​−b0​−b1​xi​)=0, ∂SSE⁡∂b1=−2∑ixi(yi−b0−b1xi)=0.\frac{\partial\operatorname{SSE}}{\partial b_1}=-2\sum_i x_i(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)=0.∂b1​∂SSE​=−2i∑​xi​(yi​−b0​−b1​xi​)=0.

The first gives b0=y‾−b1x‾b_0=\overline y-b_1\overline xb0​=y​−b1​x. Substitute into the second:

b1=∑i(xi−x‾)(yi−y‾)∑i(xi−x‾)2,b0=y‾−b1x‾.b_1=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\overline x)(y_i-\overline y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\overline x)^2},\qquad b_0=\overline y-b_1\overline x.b1​=∑i​(xi​−x)2∑i​(xi​−x)(yi​−y​)​,b0​=y​−b1​x.

The predictor must vary. With an intercept, the line passes through (x‾,y‾)(\overline x,\overline y)(x,y​) and training residuals sum to zero, up to numerical precision.

The geometry behind the normal equations

The fitted response vector lies in the span of the design columns. At the OLS solution, the residual vector is perpendicular to every column:

XTe=0.X^\mathsf T\mathbf e=\mathbf0.XTe=0.

Why? If the residual retained a component along an available design direction, moving the fit in that direction could reduce squared error. Orthogonality says that no such improvement remains within the model family.

A small hand calculation makes the formula tangible. For x=(1,2,3)x=(1,2,3)x=(1,2,3) and y=(2,3,5)y=(2,3,5)y=(2,3,5), x‾=2\overline x=2x=2, y‾=10/3\overline y=10/3y​=10/3, and the centered cross-product sum is 3. The centered predictor-square sum is 2, so

b1=1.5,b0=13.b_1=1.5,\qquad b_0=\frac13.b1​=1.5,b0​=31​.

The fitted values are 11/6,10/3,29/611/6,10/3,29/611/6,10/3,29/6, and the residuals are 1/6,−1/3,1/61/6,-1/3,1/61/6,−1/3,1/6. Their sum is zero. This identity is a property of the fitted OLS model with an intercept, not a guarantee that future errors will average to exactly zero.

3.3 Use the model for a new input

For a new input, substitute into the same fitted rule:

y^0=b0+b1x0.\widehat y_0=b_0+b_1x_0.y​0​=b0​+b1​x0​.

The next cells use x0=2200x_0=2200x0​=2200 in the dataset's distance units. This is a point prediction, not an interval. Its credibility depends on whether the fitted relationship is appropriate near that input.

Interpolation uses inputs inside the observed range; extrapolation goes outside it. Neither rescues a systematically wrong model form. Chapter 5 revisits these data using a log–log model and Kepler's third law.

A point prediction has two kinds of uncertainty

For simple regression under the classical independent, constant-variance normal-error model, estimate residual noise by s2=SSE⁡/(n−2)s^2=\operatorname{SSE}/(n-2)s2=SSE/(n−2). At input x0x_0x0​, the estimated standard error of the fitted mean is

s1n+(x0−x‾)2∑i(xi−x‾)2.s\sqrt{\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\overline x)^2}{\sum_i(x_i-\overline x)^2}}.sn1​+∑i​(xi​−x)2(x0​−x)2​​.

For a new individual response, an additional noise contribution appears:

s1+1n+(x0−x‾)2∑i(xi−x‾)2.s\sqrt{1+\frac1n+\frac{(x_0-\overline x)^2}{\sum_i(x_i-\overline x)^2}}.s1+n1​+∑i​(xi​−x)2(x0​−x)2​​.

Multiplying these standard errors by the appropriate tn−2t_{n-2}tn−2​ critical value produces the usual mean-response confidence and individual prediction intervals. The extra 1 makes the second wider. Moving away from the predictor center increases both. These formulas rely on the model; a narrow interval around a misspecified straight line can still be misleading.

3.4 Optimize by taking gradient steps

Gradient descent repeatedly steps opposite the gradient. For mean squared error,

∂J∂b0=−2n∑iei,∂J∂b1=−2n∑ixiei.\frac{\partial J}{\partial b_0}=-\frac2n\sum_i e_i,\qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial b_1}=-\frac2n\sum_i x_i e_i.∂b0​∂J​=−n2​i∑​ei​,∂b1​∂J​=−n2​i∑​xi​ei​.

With learning rate η>0\eta>0η>0, update simultaneously:

b0(t+1)=b0(t)−η∂J∂b0(b(t)),b1(t+1)=b1(t)−η∂J∂b1(b(t)).\begin{aligned}b_0^{(t+1)}&=b_0^{(t)}-\eta\frac{\partial J}{\partial b_0}(b^{(t)}),\\b_1^{(t+1)}&=b_1^{(t)}-\eta\frac{\partial J}{\partial b_1}(b^{(t)}).\end{aligned}b0(t+1)​b1(t+1)​​=b0(t)​−η∂b0​∂J​(b(t)),=b1(t)​−η∂b1​∂J​(b(t)).​

Both derivatives use iteration-ttt coefficients. A large learning rate can overshoot or diverge; a tiny rate can be slow. Different predictor scales can make optimization inefficient. Centering and scaling can improve convergence without changing the straight lines that can be represented.

The original cells use unscaled distance and η=10−8\eta=10^{-8}η=10−8. Compare final coefficients with OLS rather than assuming the iteration count is sufficient.

Read a gradient update numerically

If ∂J/∂b1=−40\partial J/\partial b_1=-40∂J/∂b1​=−40 and η=0.01\eta=0.01η=0.01, the slope update adds 0.4. The negative derivative says that a small increase in slope decreases the loss locally. Gradient descent uses that local information repeatedly.

For a full-rank quadratic objective, the Hessian is H=2XTX/nH=2X^\mathsf TX/nH=2XTX/n. A constant learning rate satisfying 0<η<2/λmax⁡(H)0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}(H)0<η<2/λmax​(H) guarantees convergence for this idealized fixed-step setting. You do not need eigenvalues to run the notebook; the formula explains why a predictor measured in very large units can force tiny stable steps.

Monitor loss, coefficient changes, and agreement with a known solution where one is available. A line that looks almost unchanged is not by itself a reliable stopping rule.

In the original code, the tiny learning rate and unscaled predictor make the intercept converge very slowly. After 10,000 iterations, this run has not reached the OLS solution; that gap is useful evidence about scaling and convergence.

3.5 Inspect the path and separate method from model

Checkpoint plots show the optimization path. For full-rank least squares, gradient descent and a direct solver target the same coefficients if optimization converges. Their finite-iteration outputs can differ substantially.

Method, model, and loss

A straight line defines a model family. Squared loss defines a fitting criterion. Gradient descent is an optimization method. Improving the optimizer cannot fix curvature the model family cannot express.

Least squares has a likelihood interpretation. For independent normal errors with common variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, the negative log likelihood, apart from constants, is

n2log⁡(σ2)+12σ2∑i(yi−y^i)2.\frac n2\log(\sigma^2)+\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2.2n​log(σ2)+2σ21​i∑​(yi​−y​i​)2.

For fixed variance, maximizing likelihood over coefficients minimizes SSE. Normality is not needed to compute OLS; it supplies a probabilistic model and classical inference.

Other losses express other priorities. Absolute loss reduces large errors' influence relative to squared loss. Regularization trades training fit against constraints on coefficients. Assess these choices using the question and new-data performance.

What a change of units does

Let z=(x−x‾)/sxz=(x-\overline x)/s_xz=(x−x)/sx​ and fit y^=c0+c1z\widehat y=c_0+c_1zy​=c0​+c1​z. In original units,

y^=(c0−c1x‾sx)+c1sxx.\widehat y=\left(c_0-\frac{c_1\overline x}{s_x}\right)+\frac{c_1}{s_x}x.y​=(c0​−sx​c1​x​)+sx​c1​​x.

Scaling does not remove the original straight-line model; it re-expresses its coefficients. This is why you can improve optimization through scaling and then interpret the fitted relationship in the original units.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import plotly.express as px
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

# Upload PlanetsData.csv first. Use all observations for this fitting example.
planets = pd.read_csv("PlanetsData.csv")
X = planets[["distance"]]
y = planets["revolution"]
model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)
planets["y_hat"] = model.predict(X)
planets["residual"] = y - planets["y_hat"]
print("Intercept:", model.intercept_, "Slope:", model.coef_[0])
print("SSE:", np.sum(planets["residual"] ** 2))

ordered = planets.sort_values("distance")
fig = px.scatter(planets, x="distance", y="revolution", hover_name="planet")
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=ordered["distance"], y=ordered["y_hat"],
                         mode="lines", name="OLS"))
fig.show()
fig = px.scatter(planets, x="y_hat", y="residual", hover_name="planet")
fig.add_hline(y=0, line_dash="dash")
fig.show()

# Gradient descent on a standardized predictor.
x = (X["distance"].to_numpy() - X["distance"].mean()) / X["distance"].std(ddof=0)
y_values = y.to_numpy()
b0, b1 = 0.0, 0.0
learning_rate = 0.05
for i in range(1000):
    error = b0 + b1 * x - y_values
    gradient_0 = 2 * error.mean()
    gradient_1 = 2 * np.mean(error * x)
    b0 = b0 - learning_rate * gradient_0
    b1 = b1 - learning_rate * gradient_1

# Convert back to the original distance units before comparing coefficients.
slope_original = b1 / X["distance"].std(ddof=0)
intercept_original = b0 - slope_original * X["distance"].mean()
print("GD coefficients on original scale:", intercept_original, slope_original)

CHECK YOUR UNDERSTANDING

Can gradient descent reach the OLS solution while the residual plot still shows a pattern?

Reveal explanation

Yes. OLS defines the objective, and gradient descent is an algorithm for optimizing it. Reaching the minimum of squared error does not guarantee that a straight-line model adequately represents the relationship.

Optional practice questions

Want more practice? Try these questions after reading the chapter. They are for self-study; work through your reasoning before revealing an answer.

Reveal 5 practice questions

  1. Read the residuals

    A least-squares line for the planets data has a large R², but its residuals form a curved pattern. Has OLS failed to minimize SSE? What has the pattern actually revealed?

    Reveal suggested answer

    OLS can correctly minimize SSE within the class of straight lines while that class misses an important curved relationship. The pattern diagnoses an inadequate mean function, not necessarily an optimization failure. A high R² does not guarantee that the chosen model captures the structure.

  2. Take one gradient step

    For MSE = (1/n)Σ(yᵢ − b₀ − b₁xᵢ)², take n = 2, x = [1, 2], y = [2, 4], and initial b₀ = b₁ = 0. Compute both partial derivatives and update both coefficients simultaneously with learning rate 0.1.

    Reveal suggested answer

    Initially the residuals are [2, 4]. The intercept derivative is −(2/2)(2 + 4) = −6; the slope derivative is −(2/2)(1×2 + 2×4) = −10. Subtracting 0.1 times each gradient gives b₀ = 0.6 and b₁ = 1.0. Both derivatives use the old coefficient values. The new MSE is 1.06, compared with the initial MSE of 10.

  3. When is the OLS solution unique?

    A regression includes an intercept and two predictor columns, with the second exactly twice the first. Why is XᵀX not invertible? Could fitted training values still be uniquely determined?

    Reveal suggested answer

    The columns are linearly dependent, so the design matrix lacks full column rank and XᵀX is singular. Multiple coefficient vectors can represent the same fitted vector. The least-squares projection, and therefore the fitted training values, is still unique even though the individual coefficients are not.

  4. Diagnose gradient descent

    Gradient descent oscillates and the loss grows after each update. Give one likely numerical cause and a useful change. If the algorithm instead converges very slowly on differently scaled predictors, what would you inspect?

    Reveal suggested answer

    The learning rate may be too large; reduce it and verify that the gradient and update signs are correct. For slow convergence, inspect predictor scales and the loss trajectory. Standardization can improve the geometry of the optimization. A fixed iteration count alone is not evidence of convergence.

  5. Explain bootstrap coefficients

    You resample the observed rows, refit the regression, and record the slope 2,000 times. What does the spread of those slopes estimate? Why does a narrow distribution not prove that the fitted straight line is scientifically correct?

    Reveal suggested answer

    Under suitable sampling and bootstrap assumptions, the spread approximates the slope estimator's sampling variability. It reflects stability of this fitting procedure under resampling. A stable estimator can still be biased or use a misspecified mean function, and bootstrapping does not turn an observational association into a causal effect.
