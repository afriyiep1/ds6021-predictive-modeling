5.1 Question the common-slope assumption

An additive model assumes that the effect of one predictor does not depend on another predictor. For BMI xxx and smoking indicator SSS,

y^=b0+b1x+b2S.\widehat y=b_0+b_1x+b_2S.y​=b0​+b1​x+b2​S.

For nonsmokers, y^=b0+b1x\widehat y=b_0+b_1xy​=b0​+b1​x; for smokers, y^=(b0+b2)+b1x\widehat y=(b_0+b_2)+b_1xy​=(b0​+b2​)+b1​x. Both lines have slope b1b_1b1​. The group difference is the constant b2b_2b2​ at every BMI.

The first notebook cells plot the two groups. Ask whether a constant vertical gap is plausible before fitting a common-slope model. A pattern can motivate a richer model, but the visual difference alone does not establish a causal effect of smoking or BMI.

An additive model makes a testable restriction

For a fixed BMI increase hhh, the additive model predicts the same change b1hb_1hb1​h in both groups. Compare fitted group slopes with the scatterplot: if charges rise much faster with BMI in one group, the common-slope restriction may discard important structure.

Adding a category indicator adjusts the intercept. It does not, by itself, adjust a numerical predictor's slope. The distinction is visible in the design matrix: a column containing only zeros and ones cannot replace a column containing BMI multiplied by that indicator.

5.2 Add and interpret the interaction

Add the product of BMI and smoking status as another predictor:

y^=b0+b1x+b2S+b3xS.\widehat y=b_0+b_1x+b_2S+b_3xS.y​=b0​+b1​x+b2​S+b3​xS.

Substituting the indicator values makes the interpretation clear:

S=0:y^=b0+b1x,S=1:y^=(b0+b2)+(b1+b3)x.\begin{aligned}S=0:\quad\widehat y&=b_0+b_1x,\\S=1:\quad\widehat y&=(b_0+b_2)+(b_1+b_3)x.\end{aligned}S=0:y​S=1:y​​=b0​+b1​x,=(b0​+b2​)+(b1​+b3​)x.​

Thus b1b_1b1​ is the nonsmoker slope, b1+b3b_1+b_3b1​+b3​ is the smoker slope, and b3b_3b3​ is the difference between slopes. The coefficient b2b_2b2​ is the smoker–nonsmoker gap at BMI zero; that reference point may be scientifically unhelpful.

The design matrix simply gains a column containing xSxSxS. This is still a linear model in its coefficients, even though it contains a product of predictors. The next cells construct that column, fit the model, and display the group lines.

Interpret an interaction as a difference of differences

For a BMI increase hhh, the predicted changes are b1hb_1hb1​h for nonsmokers and (b1+b3)h(b_1+b_3)h(b1​+b3​)h for smokers. Subtracting gives

[y^(x+h,1)−y^(x,1)]−[y^(x+h,0)−y^(x,0)]=b3h.[\widehat y(x+h,1)-\widehat y(x,1)]-[\widehat y(x+h,0)-\widehat y(x,0)]=b_3h.[y​(x+h,1)−y​(x,1)]−[y​(x+h,0)−y​(x,0)]=b3​h.

At h=1h=1h=1, the interaction coefficient is a difference between one-unit BMI associations. This algebra gives meaning to the product column and explains why interpreting b3b_3b3​ as a stand-alone smoker effect is incorrect.

The calculation remains an association in these observational data. A model term named “interaction” does not establish a causal mechanism.

5.3 Make a conditional comparison

Compare groups at a meaningful value

At BMI xxx, the fitted smoker–nonsmoker difference is

Δ(x)=b2+b3x.\Delta(x)=b_2+b_3x.Δ(x)=b2​+b3​x.

For the lecture's illustrative coefficients b0=−5000b_0=-5000b0​=−5000, b1=200b_1=200b1​=200, b2=−20000b_2=-20000b2​=−20000, and b3=1400b_3=1400b3​=1400, the difference at BMI 30 is −20000+1400(30)=22000-20000+1400(30)=22000−20000+1400(30)=22000. The negative main-effect coefficient does not imply that the smoker line is lower throughout the observed range.

Centering at a meaningful value, xc=x−30x_c=x-30xc​=x−30, makes the indicator coefficient the gap at BMI 30. This changes the interpretation of coefficients while preserving fitted values when all relevant terms are included.

Continuous interactions and hierarchy

For two continuous predictors,

y^=b0+b1x+b2z+b3xz,∂y^∂x=b1+b3z.\widehat y=b_0+b_1x+b_2z+b_3xz,\qquad \frac{\partial\widehat y}{\partial x}=b_1+b_3z.y​=b0​+b1​x+b2​z+b3​xz,∂x∂y​​=b1​+b3​z.

The effect of xxx depends on zzz. The usual hierarchy principle retains both main effects when their interaction is included. It makes the model's meaning coherent and avoids imposing unintended constraints at zero. Always state the value of the other predictor when interpreting a conditional slope.

Centering changes the reference question

Write x=xc+cx=x_c+cx=xc​+c in the interaction model and collect terms:

y^=(b0+b1c)+b1xc+(b2+b3c)S+b3xcS.\widehat y=(b_0+b_1c)+b_1x_c+(b_2+b_3c)S+b_3x_cS.y​=(b0​+b1​c)+b1​xc​+(b2​+b3​c)S+b3​xc​S.

The smoking coefficient becomes the group difference at BMI ccc. The interaction coefficient and fitted values remain unchanged. Choosing c=30c=30c=30 can make the main-effect interpretation more useful than choosing zero.

Conditional comparisons also require overlap. If one group has almost no observations near a selected BMI, the fitted gap relies heavily on extrapolation. Restrict plotted group lines to their observed ranges and explain where comparisons are weakly supported.

5.4 Diagnose a transformation: the original scale

Return to the planets data. On the original scale, a straight line may leave curved residual structure because the relationship between orbital distance and period is closer to a power law.

A transformation changes both model interpretation and the fitting criterion. Fitting a model to log⁡Y\log YlogY minimizes squared errors in log units. It gives a different relative emphasis to large and small responses than minimizing squared errors in the original units.

Start with the original-scale scatterplot, fitted line, and residuals. Then compare the transformed model on the scale used for fitting. A visually smoother plot is useful evidence, but model choice should also consider the science and the prediction task.

Compare errors on the question's scale

Original-scale OLS gives greater absolute weight to errors for long-period bodies. Log-scale OLS emphasizes discrepancies in ratios: predicting twice the observed period produces the same log error regardless of the period's size.

When choosing between these models, calculate an error measure on a common response scale if the decision concerns original periods. Do not select a model merely because its R2R^2R2 is larger after transforming the response: the total variation in the denominator has changed.

A transformation should have a rationale in the data, the scientific relationship, or the prediction loss. Here, a power law provides a particularly clear scientific motivation.

A visible residual pattern can motivate a change in model form. A transformation is a modeling choice that changes the loss and interpretation.

5.5 Refit and interpret on the log–log scale

For positive values, a power relationship becomes linear after taking logarithms:

y=Axb⟺log⁡y=log⁡A+blog⁡x.y=A x^b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log y=\log A+b\log x.y=Axb⟺logy=logA+blogx.

In the fitted log–log model,

log⁡y^=a+blog⁡x,y~=exp⁡(a)xb.\widehat{\log y}=a+b\log x,\qquad \widetilde y=\exp(a)x^b.logy​=a+blogx,y​=exp(a)xb.

The slope bbb is an elasticity: a small 1% increase in xxx corresponds to approximately a bbb% increase in the exponentiated fitted curve. For a finite proportional increase rrr, the exact percentage change is

100[(1+r)b−1]%.100\big[(1+r)^b-1\big]\%.100[(1+r)b−1]%.

The variables must be positive. Zeros and negative values need a scientifically justified treatment, not an arbitrary log operation. Using the same log base for both variables leaves the slope unchanged.

Exponentiating a predicted log response is not automatically the arithmetic conditional mean of YYY. Under normal log errors with constant variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, that mean includes the factor exp⁡(σ2/2)\exp(\sigma^2/2)exp(σ2/2). Without such assumptions, retransformation needs separate attention.

In log(y) = a + b log(x), a small 1% increase in x corresponds to approximately b% change in the exponentiated fitted curve. Exponentiating a fitted log response is not automatically an estimate of the arithmetic conditional mean.

Kepler’s third law explains the slope

For bodies orbiting the same dominant central mass, Kepler's third law relates orbital period TTT to the orbit's semimajor axis aorba_{\mathrm{orb}}aorb​:

T2=Caorb3.T^2=C a_{\mathrm{orb}}^3.T2=Caorb3​.

The semimajor axis describes the size of an elliptical orbit; it is not the body's instantaneous distance from the Sun. Taking logarithms gives

2log⁡T=log⁡C+3log⁡aorb,log⁡T=12log⁡C+32log⁡aorb.\begin{aligned}2\log T&=\log C+3\log a_{\mathrm{orb}},\\\log T&=\frac12\log C+\frac32\log a_{\mathrm{orb}}.\end{aligned}2logTlogT​=logC+3logaorb​,=21​logC+23​logaorb​.​

So the physics predicts a log–log slope of 1.5. The tabulated distance values in the course file serve as the orbital-distance approximation for this exercise. Fitting all nine supplied rows gives a slope of 1.5013, remarkably close to the predicted value. The natural-log intercept is −0.9031 in the file's units; unlike the slope, that intercept changes when distance or time units change.

A useful unit-free version normalizes to Earth:

TTE≈(aorbaE)3/2.\frac{T}{T_E}\approx\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{orb}}}{a_E}\right)^{3/2}.TE​T​≈(aE​aorb​​)3/2.

Doubling orbital size multiplies the predicted period by 23/2≈2.8282^{3/2}\approx2.82823/2≈2.828. A 10% increase gives about a 15.37% increase in period, while the small-change approximation gives 15%. The empirical fit is consistent with the physical relationship; fitting a line to rounded tabulated values is not a proof of the law.

Read NASA's explanation of Kepler's laws.

From a fitted exponent to a scientific interpretation

The power-law exponent can also be written as

b=dlog⁡ydlog⁡x=xydydx.b=\frac{d\log y}{d\log x}=\frac{x}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}.b=dlogxdlogy​=yx​dxdy​.

Thus elasticity measures a relative response change per relative predictor change. The original-scale slope dy/dx=bAxb−1dy/dx=bAx^{b-1}dy/dx=bAxb−1 changes with distance even when the log–log slope is constant. For the Kepler curve, longer-distance increments correspond to increasingly large period increments, while the proportional relationship remains stable.

Book connection: ISL and Practical Statistics extend regression through products and transformed predictors; Molnar asks how those extensions change interpretation. The planets example supplies the scientific meaning of a fitted exponent, while the insurance example supplies the meaning of a conditional slope.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

planets = pd.read_csv("PlanetsData.csv")
# The file includes Pluto as a historical row: use all nine listed bodies.
assert (planets[["distance", "revolution"]] > 0).all().all()
log_distance = np.log(planets[["distance"]])
log_period = np.log(planets["revolution"])
model = LinearRegression().fit(log_distance, log_period)
print(f"Fitted slope: {model.coef_[0]:.4f}")
print("Kepler's predicted slope: 1.5000")
print(f"Natural-log intercept in the file's units: {model.intercept_:.4f}")

# Normalize to Earth to compare without needing the file's physical units.
earth = planets.loc[planets["planet"] == "Earth"].iloc[0]
x = np.log(planets["distance"] / earth["distance"])
y = np.log(planets["revolution"] / earth["revolution"])
grid = np.linspace(x.min(), x.max(), 200)
normalized_intercept = (
    model.intercept_ + model.coef_[0] * np.log(earth["distance"])
    - np.log(earth["revolution"])
)
fig = go.Figure()
fig.add_scatter(x=x, y=y, mode="markers", text=planets["planet"],
               name="Course data", marker=dict(size=9, color="#232d4b"),
               hovertemplate="%{text}<br>log distance ratio: %{x:.3f}"
                             "<br>log period ratio: %{y:.3f}<extra></extra>")
fig.add_scatter(x=grid, y=normalized_intercept + model.coef_[0] * grid,
               mode="lines", name="Fitted slope 1.5013",
               line=dict(color="#276582", width=3))
fig.add_scatter(x=grid, y=1.5 * grid, mode="lines", name="Kepler slope 1.5",
               line=dict(color="#cf591d", width=2, dash="dash"))
fig.update_layout(template="plotly_white", title="A physical law becomes a straight line",
                  xaxis_title="log(distance / Earth's distance)",
                  yaxis_title="log(period / Earth's period)",
                  legend=dict(orientation="h", y=-0.25), margin=dict(l=65,r=25,t=60,b=100))
fig.show()

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import plotly.express as px
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

# Upload insurance.csv and PlanetsData.csv.
insurance = pd.read_csv("insurance.csv")
insurance["S"] = (insurance["smoker"] == "yes").astype(int)
insurance["bmi_smoker"] = insurance["bmi"] * insurance["S"]
y = insurance["charges"]

for name, columns in {
    "Additive": ["bmi", "S"],
    "Interaction": ["bmi", "S", "bmi_smoker"]
}.items():
    model = LinearRegression().fit(insurance[columns], y)
    print(name, "Intercept:", model.intercept_)
    print(pd.Series(model.coef_, index=columns))
    fig = px.scatter(insurance, x="bmi", y="charges", color="smoker",
                     opacity=0.4, title=name)
    for s, label in [(0, "no"), (1, "yes")]:
        group = insurance[insurance["S"] == s].sort_values("bmi")
        fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=group["bmi"],
                                 y=model.predict(group[columns]),
                                 mode="lines", name=f"Fitted: {label}"))
    fig.show()

planets = pd.read_csv("PlanetsData.csv")
# All distance and revolution values in this file are positive.
planets["log_distance"] = np.log(planets["distance"])
planets["log_revolution"] = np.log(planets["revolution"])
log_model = LinearRegression().fit(planets[["log_distance"]],
                                    planets["log_revolution"])
planets["fitted_log"] = log_model.predict(planets[["log_distance"]])
print("Log–log slope:", log_model.coef_[0])
print("Exact % curve change for 10% more distance:",
      100 * (1.10 ** log_model.coef_[0] - 1))
fig = px.scatter(planets, x="log_distance", y="log_revolution", hover_name="planet")
fig.add_trace(go.Scatter(x=planets["log_distance"], y=planets["fitted_log"],
                         mode="lines", name="Log–log fit"))
fig.show()
fig = px.scatter(x=planets["fitted_log"],
                 y=planets["log_revolution"] - planets["fitted_log"],
                 labels={"x": "Fitted log(revolution)", "y": "Log residual"})
fig.add_hline(y=0, line_dash="dash")
fig.show()

With an interaction, is the smoking coefficient a constant smoker–nonsmoker difference?

Reveal explanation

No. With BMI × smoking in the model, the group difference at BMI x is b₂ + b₃x. The coefficient b₂ is the difference at BMI = 0, which is outside a meaningful clinical range. Centering BMI changes the reference point.

Optional practice questions

Want more practice? Try these questions after reading the chapter. They are for self-study; work through your reasoning before revealing an answer.

Reveal 5 practice questions

  1. Work through an interaction

    Suppose ŷ = −5,000 + 200x − 20,000S + 1,400xS, where x is BMI and S = 1 denotes a smoker. Find each group's BMI slope and the smoker–nonsmoker difference at BMI 30. Why is the coefficient −20,000 easy to misinterpret?

    Reveal suggested answer

    The nonsmoker slope is 200 and the smoker slope is 200 + 1,400 = 1,600. At BMI 30, the group difference is −20,000 + 1,400×30 = 22,000. The −20,000 coefficient is the group difference at BMI zero, not a constant difference across BMI values; BMI zero is outside a meaningful comparison here.

  2. Center for interpretation

    Rewrite the preceding interaction using x_c = x − 30. What are the new intercept and smoking-indicator coefficient? Does centering change the predicted charges?

    Reveal suggested answer

    Substitute x = x_c + 30. The new model is ŷ = 1,000 + 200x_c + 22,000S + 1,400x_cS. The intercept is the nonsmoker prediction at BMI 30; the smoking coefficient is the group gap there. The predictions are identical because this is an algebraic reparameterization with all relevant terms retained.

  3. Use a log–log slope

    A fitted log–log model has slope 1.5. How does the exponentiated fitted response change when x increases by 1%? What is the exact percentage change for a 10% increase, and why is it not exactly 15%?

    Reveal suggested answer

    A 1% increase corresponds to approximately a 1.5% increase for a small change. For a 10% increase, the exact change is 100[(1.10)^1.5 − 1] ≈ 15.37%. The elasticity approximation is local; a finite change follows the power function.

  4. Connect the model to Kepler

    Starting with T² = Ca³, derive the expected slope in a regression of log orbital period on log orbital size for bodies orbiting the same dominant mass. What happens to the slope if distance units change?

    Reveal suggested answer

    Taking logs gives 2 log T = log C + 3 log a, hence log T = (1/2) log C + (3/2) log a. The expected slope is 1.5. A multiplicative change of distance units adds a constant to log a, changing the intercept but not the slope. Orbital size refers to the semimajor axis, approximated by the distance variable in this exercise.

  5. Retransform carefully

    A model predicts log charges and you exponentiate the predicted log value. Is this automatically an estimate of arithmetic mean charges? Explain the distinction and the role of residual variation.

    Reveal suggested answer

    No: in general exp(E[log Y | X]) differs from E[Y | X]. Exponentiation gives a geometric-mean-scale prediction and, under suitable symmetric log-error assumptions, a conditional median. With normal log errors of constant variance σ², the arithmetic mean includes an exp(σ²/2) factor. Other error structures require a suitable retransformation method; residual variability matters.
